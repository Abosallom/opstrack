Giving your app its own
web address

Right now your tracker lives at a long address that belongs to something else. You have bought nphiescore.com. This guide takes you through the one job that makes that address point at your app — nine small entries typed into a form at Hostinger.

You do not need to understand how any of it works. Every term is explained on page 2, every entry is shown filled in, and page 9 checks your work for you.

Today
Someone types nphiescore.com and lands on a Hostinger holding page. Your app is somewhere else entirely.
⟶
After
Someone types nphiescore.com and the internet knows to fetch your app from GitHub, where it is published.

You genuinely cannot break anything today

Nobody uses nphiescore.com yet — not you, not Nasser, not the app. The only thing living at that address is a holding page you are replacing. If you type something wrong, you fix the row and try again. There is nothing here to lose and nothing to back up first.

Why this is worth doing before anything else

Two reasons. First, it is slow — not your part, which is fifteen minutes, but the part afterwards where the rest of the internet learns the new answer. That happens on its own, in the background, and it may take a few hours. Starting it early means it is finished by the time anything else needs it. Second, it is a genuine safety fix: your app currently shares its web address with three unrelated little apps, and sharing an address means sharing the place browsers keep your sign-in. Its own address ends that.

The words you will see

Six of them, and then they stop being mysterious. Come back here whenever one turns up.
DNS
The internet's phone book. Every website address has to be looked up in it to find the actual machine that answers. Today nphiescore.com is in the book with the wrong number; you are correcting the entry.
A recordtype: A
One line in the phone book: this name → this number. You will add four of them, all for the same name, because GitHub answers on four different machines and wants all four listed.
AAAA recordtype: AAAA
Exactly the same idea, for the newer, longer style of internet address — the ones with colons in them. Some phones and networks can only use these, so you list both kinds. Said aloud: “quad A”.
CNAME recordtype: CNAME
A forwarding note rather than a number: “don't ask me, go and look up this other name instead.” You will use exactly one, so that www.nphiescore.com works as well as the plain version.
@the Name field
Shorthand for “the domain itself, with nothing in front” — that is, nphiescore.com rather than www.nphiescore.com or mail.nphiescore.com. Eight of your nine entries use @.
TTLa number of seconds
How long other computers are allowed to remember an answer before asking again. 3600 is one hour. It is the least important field on the form — whatever it is set to, the entry works.

And one thing that is not a word you need — “propagation”

You will see this word if you search for help. All it means is: the rest of the world is still remembering the old answer and has not asked again yet. It sorts itself out. Nothing is wrong, nothing needs fixing, and there is no button that makes it go faster.

The two colons together are correct — do not “tidy” them

In this style of address, :: is a deliberate shorthand meaning “a run of zeros goes here”. 2606:50c0:8000::153 is complete and correct exactly as printed. If you change it to a single colon, the form will happily accept it and it will simply never work. Copy character for character, including the ::153 at the end.

Why bother with a second set at all

Mobile networks increasingly hand out only this newer kind of address. A phone on such a network will ask for the AAAA entry, find nothing, and give up — so the app would work for some of your team and not others, with nothing on screen to explain why. Four more rows now prevents a fault that is very hard to be told about.

Read the “Points to” value twice before you press Add

It is abosallom.github.io. — the GitHub address, not your own domain. This is the single most common way this job goes wrong, because the obvious guess is to point www at nphiescore.com — which is exactly what the entry you deleted on page 5 was doing. It has to name GitHub, because GitHub is what actually answers.

There is no https:// and no /opstrack in this field. Just the name.

The full stop at the end

abosallom.github.io. — note the dot after io. It means “that is the complete name, do not add my domain onto the end of it”. Some forms need it, others ignore it, and Hostinger may add or remove it for you when you save. Either way is fine and page 9 will catch it if it went wrong.

That is the whole job at Hostinger

Nine entries. You do not need to buy anything, enable anything, or use Hostinger's “connect a website” wizard — that would point the domain at Hostinger's own hosting, which is the opposite of what you have just set up.

Step 5 · the www entry8 / 10
6

Check your work

Two ways to do this. Pick whichever you prefer — they answer the same question.

The easy way — in a browser

Go to dnschecker.org, type nphiescore.com in the box, choose A from the dropdown, and press search. You are looking for the four numbers ending 108, 109, 110, 111. Then change the dropdown to AAAA, and finally to CNAME with www.nphiescore.com in the box.

The precise way — in Terminal

Press ⌘ + Space, type Terminal, press Enter. A window with text in it opens. Paste these one line at a time, pressing Enter after each:

$ dig +short nphiescore.com A 185.199.108.153 185.199.109.153 185.199.110.153 185.199.111.153 $ dig +short nphiescore.com AAAA 2606:50c0:8000::153 (and the other three) $ dig +short www.nphiescore.com CNAME abosallom.github.io.

The order the lines come back in does not matter — it shuffles deliberately. What matters is the count: four, four, and one.

If you get nothing back, or still see 2.57.91.91

Nothing is wrong. Other computers were told to remember the old answer for four hours, and that time has not run out yet. Make a cup of tea, or leave it until tomorrow, and run it again. There is nothing to fix and no way to hurry it.

Do not judge this by visiting the website

https://nphiescore.com will not show your app yet, and that is correct. GitHub does not know about the domain until I publish the app to it, which is a later step and mine. Expect an error page. The checks above are the whole test.

I publish the app to the new address and check it came up properly. Then three short jobs come back to you, and I will write them up the same way as this: telling Supabase about the new address so sign-in emails point at the right place, confirming ownership of the domain with GitHub, and switching on the padlock once GitHub has finished issuing the certificate — which can take up to a day and is normal.

One thing to expect, so it is not a surprise

When the app moves to the new address, you and Nasser will be signed out once and will need to sign in again. If either of you has added the app to a phone home screen, that icon will need removing and adding again from the new address. Your accounts and passwords are untouched — it is only the browser forgetting where it put things. This is exactly why we are moving now, while it affects two people rather than eighteen.

And if you change your mind, it all comes back

Every part of this is reversible. The app can be put back at its old address whenever you want, and these entries can stay where they are in the meantime, pointing at nothing, costing nothing.
